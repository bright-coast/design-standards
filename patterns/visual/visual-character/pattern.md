## Rule
<p>Every generator has a default look, and a screen left on it reads as machine-made whatever this year&rsquo;s default happens to be. Character comes from making one explicit decision on each of four axes, written down once (in the <a href="#lp-design-contract">Project Design Contract</a>, entry 32) and applied everywhere. This entry does not choose a style; it rules out not choosing. <strong>Type: where does the contrast live?</strong> Either high contrast (display type at 2.5 times the body size or more, a hard weight jump, or a second family) or deliberately low contrast for a dense expert tool; the comfortable middle is no decision. The one family versus two families choice is held as two variants in <a href="#lp-typography-system">Typography as a System</a> (entry 24). <strong>Colour: what is the signature?</strong> One signature colour, picked for the product&rsquo;s temperament and used rarely enough to mean something: the primary action and the one thing that must be found. Its contrast is decided with it: label text on the signature clears 4.5:1, and the signature clears 3:1 against the surface behind it, or the lightness changes until it does. Neutrals are calibrated warm, cool or pure to match the temperament, never the framework&rsquo;s default grey ramp, and status colours stay semantic.</p>
<p><strong>Space: what is the rhythm?</strong> Dense and efficient or airy and editorial, chosen once and then applied everywhere, with compression inside groups and release between them; uniform gaps of about 16px everywhere are the tell of undecided space. <strong>Finish: what is the separation language?</strong> One primary way for surfaces to separate: borders, shadows or flat colour planes. All three on one screen means no decision was made. Finish also lives in consistent radii, optical alignment and real hover, focus and active states. Two checks before shipping. Strip the accent: the screen should still work in neutrals alone, because hierarchy is not colour&rsquo;s job. Then the lineup test: would this screenshot be recognisable among ten generated screens for the same prompt? If not, walk the four axes and find the one still sitting on its default.</p>
<p><strong>Start from the product&rsquo;s world.</strong> Before the four axes, name three concrete things the product&rsquo;s users handle, read or look at all day (a workshop: work orders, parts bins, service bays; payroll: ledgers, payslips, stamps). The family, the signature colour and one memorable element per screen come from those words, not from taste; everything around that one element stays quiet, and on a 1280px desktop the screen is composed (a header band, two columns, display-size type) rather than one narrow column on an empty page. <strong>The font has to load.</strong> Point at the line that loads each family: the project&rsquo;s own font pipeline, a framework font loader, an installed font package, an @font-face with the file in the repo or, with none of those, a Google Fonts or Bunny Fonts link with preconnect and display=swap, always with a named fallback stack. A bare system stack is the operating system&rsquo;s default, kept only when the project already uses it or the owner asks for it. In an existing project, read its font setup first and change it only when a new look was asked for. <strong>Check it on a clean machine:</strong> after wiring up a font, confirm it renders on a computer that does not have it installed, or with local fonts ruled out, and see the font file load in the network panel. <strong>Monospace is for codes, IDs and keys only;</strong> figures stay in the text face with tabular-nums.</p>
<p><strong>Colour on text, and the danger rule.</strong> Any text in a colour clears 4.5:1 on what sits behind it: a word or a count in the signature, and a status tag&rsquo;s text on its own tint. A signature that only reaches 3:1 is a fill, a dot or a rule, never text. The signature must not read as danger: red and orange-red (hue under about 30 degrees) mark failures and destructive actions in a work tool, so a signature in that range fills no primary button, and any signature keeps its hue at least 40 degrees away from the danger colour. <strong>The drift check.</strong> Generators fall into two default looks. The first is the system sans stack, monospace for figures and small labels, a warm off-white page (around #f6f5f2), 4px radii and a 1px border around every group; three of those five on one screen means it was not decided. The second appears once fonts load: the same few safe families on every product (IBM Plex Sans with Plex Mono, condensed headings, a soft display serif on every title) and a burnt orange signature (around #c2410c) a few degrees from the danger red. They are good faces, but reached for every time they become the new default, so pick from the product&rsquo;s world first and then check against this list. <strong>Read the result back from the code, not from memory:</strong> list the sizes and weights used, point at each font-loading line, count the monospace uses, count where the signature appears (more than the primary action plus one thing to find is decoration), list the distinct gap values, count the separation devices, and search for framework palette names and raw hex values, because each hit is a default that slipped through. On a phone, headings and large figures stay around 28 to 32px under 640px, so a figure and its sign stay on one line.</p>
<p><strong>Name the default, then leave it.</strong> Before building, write one line describing the screen any similar app would get (a white card grid, a blue primary button, a row of stat tiles across the top). Then change every part of the plan that matches that line. Compute every contrast ratio with a tool or the formula, never by eye, because a colour that looks readable often is not.</p>
## Do
- One written decision per axis: type, colour, space, finish
- Label text on the signature at 4.5:1, the signature itself at 3:1 against its surface
- The screen still works with the accent stripped out
- A loading line for every font family, with a fallback stack
- The web font checked on a machine that does not have it installed
- Monospace only on codes, IDs and keys; figures in the text face with tabular-nums
- One line describing the screen any similar app would get, and every matching part of the plan changed
- Contrast ratios computed with a tool
## Avoid
- Borders, shadows and flat planes all separating surfaces on one screen
- Uniform 16px gaps everywhere
- A style adopted because it was the default, with no stated reason
- A red or orange-red signature filling the primary button
- Three of the five default-look tells on one screen
- Contrast judged by eye
## Extra: variant-note
<div class="variant-note"><b>Red alerts versus a domain alert colour, two variants.</b> Variant A: red or orange-red marks failures, as above. Use when: the default, for any work tool. Variant B: an alert colour from the product&rsquo;s own palette (a deep magenta, a strong amber). Use when: red already means something else in the product&rsquo;s world. It stays 40 degrees of hue from the signature, clears 3:1 (4.5:1 as text) and always comes with an icon or a word (Failed, Low). Both kept on purpose.</div>
